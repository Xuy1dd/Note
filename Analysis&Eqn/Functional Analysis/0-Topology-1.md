
# 1 Topological Spaces

> [!example] Example 1.1
> On $k^n$, the Zariski closed sets are given by common zero sets of polynomials. For any point
> $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$,
> $$
> \{a\}=\bigcap_{j=1}^n\{x:x_j-a_j=0\},
> $$
> every singleton is closed; hence the Zariski topology is $T_1$.
>
> If $k$ is a finite field, then $k^n$ is finite. Every subset is a finite union of closed singletons and is therefore closed; by taking complements, every subset is also open. Thus the Zariski topology is discrete and hence Hausdorff.
>
> If $k$ is an infinite field, write $D(P)=\{x:P(x)\ne0\}$ for a basic open set. For nonzero polynomials $P,Q$,
> $$
> D(P)\cap D(Q)=D(PQ).
> $$
> Since $k[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ is an integral domain, $PQ\ne0$. Moreover, because $k$ is infinite, a nonzero polynomial cannot vanish identically on all of $k^n$, so $D(PQ)\ne\varnothing$. Consequently, any two nonempty Zariski open sets intersect. Distinct points therefore cannot be separated by disjoint open neighborhoods, and the topology is not Hausdorff.
>
> The key distinction is that on a finite space, $T_1$ implies discreteness; on an infinite space, $T_1$ generally implies neither discreteness nor the Hausdorff property.
> ^ex-zariski-topology

# 2 Continuous Maps

> [!definition] Definition 2.1
> Let $X,Y$ be topological spaces and let $f:X\to Y$.
>
> - The map $f$ is **continuous** if $f^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$ for every open set $V\subseteq Y$. Equivalently, the inverse image of every closed set is closed.
> - The map $f$ is **continuous at $x\in X$** if, for every neighborhood $V$ of $f(x)$, there is a neighborhood $U$ of $x$ such that $f(U)\subseteq V$.
> - A composition of continuous maps is continuous.
> ^def-continuous-map

> [!proposition] Proposition 2.1
> A map $f:X\to Y$ is continuous if and only if it is continuous at every point of $X$.
>
> **Proof idea.** Apply the global and pointwise criteria in [[#^def-continuous-map]]. Global continuity immediately implies continuity at every point. Conversely, if $V\subseteq Y$ is open, then $f^{-1}(V)$ is a neighborhood of each of its points and is therefore open.
> ^prop-global-pointwise-continuity

> [!proposition] Proposition 2.2
> If the topology on $Y$ is generated by a family $\mathcal E$, then $f:X\to Y$ is continuous if and only if $f^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$ for every $V\in\mathcal E$.
>
> **Proof idea.** By the continuity criterion in [[#^def-continuous-map]], it is enough to check the generators because the inverse-image operation preserves arbitrary unions and finite intersections.
> ^prop-continuity-on-generators

------------------------
> [!definition] Definition 2.2
> A bijection $f:X\to Y$ is a **homeomorphism** if both $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are continuous. Homeomorphic spaces have the same topological properties, just as isomorphic algebraic structures have the same algebraic properties.
> ^def-homeomorphism

> [!definition] Definition 2.3
> An injective map $f:X\to Y$ is an **embedding** if $f:X\to f(X)$ is a homeomorphism in the sense of [[#^def-homeomorphism]], where $f(X)$ is equipped with the relative topology inherited from $Y$.
> ^def-embedding

> [!definition] Definition 2.4
> Given a family of maps $f_\alpha:X\to Y_\alpha$, the **weak topology** generated by them is the weakest topology on $X$ for which every $f_\alpha$ is continuous. It is generated by the sets
> $$
> f_\alpha^{-1}(U_\alpha),\qquad U_\alpha\subseteq Y_\alpha\text{ open}.
> $$
> ^def-weak-topology

> [!definition] Definition 2.5
> Let
> $$
> X=\prod_{\alpha\in A}X_\alpha,
> $$
> and let $\pi_\alpha:X\to X_\alpha$ be the coordinate projection. The **product topology** is the weak topology from [[#^def-weak-topology]] generated by all the projections $\pi_\alpha$.
>
> A basis for the product topology is given by sets of the form
> $$
> \bigcap_{j=1}^n\pi_{\alpha_j}^{-1}(U_j).
> $$
> Thus a basic open set in the product topology restricts only finitely many coordinates. Equivalently, a product set $\prod_{\alpha\in A}U_\alpha$ is basic open only when $U_\alpha=X_\alpha$ for all but finitely many $\alpha$.
> ^def-product-topology

> [!proposition] Proposition 2.3
> If every $X_\alpha$ is Hausdorff, then $\prod_{\alpha\in A}X_\alpha$ is Hausdorff.
>
> **Proof idea.** Using the coordinate projections from [[#^def-product-topology]], observe that two distinct points in the product must differ in at least one coordinate. Separate those two coordinate values in that factor space, and then take inverse images under the corresponding projection to obtain disjoint neighborhoods in the product.
> ^prop-product-hausdorff

> [!proposition] Proposition 2.4
> A map
> $$
> f:Y\to\prod_{\alpha\in A}X_\alpha
> $$
> is continuous if and only if every coordinate map $\pi_\alpha\circ f:Y\to X_\alpha$ is continuous.
>
> **Proof idea.** One direction follows from the continuity of compositions. For the converse, apply [[#^prop-continuity-on-generators]] to the generating sets in [[#^def-product-topology]].
> ^prop-map-into-product

> [!proposition] Proposition 2.5
> If all coordinate spaces are equal to the same topological space $X$, then
> $$
> X^A=\{f:A\to X\}=\prod_{\alpha\in A}X.
> $$
> For a sequence of functions $\{f_n\}\subseteq X^A$,
> $$
> f_n\to f\text{ in the product topology}
> \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
> f_n(\alpha)\to f(\alpha)\text{ for every }\alpha\in A.
> $$
> Therefore, the product topology on $X^A$ is precisely the **topology of pointwise convergence**.
>
> **Proof idea.** By the basis description in [[#^def-product-topology]], a basic neighborhood of $f$ imposes conditions on only finitely many values $f(\alpha_1),\ldots,f(\alpha_k)$. Under pointwise convergence, choose an index $N_j$ for each coordinate and then take $N=\max_jN_j$ so that all finitely many conditions hold simultaneously. Conversely, fix $\alpha$ and use the basic neighborhood $\pi_\alpha^{-1}(U)$ to obtain $f_n(\alpha)\to f(\alpha)$.
>
> **Key point.** The product topology controls only finitely many coordinates at a time, so it describes pointwise convergence rather than uniform convergence.
> ^prop-product-pointwise-convergence

> [!lemma] Lemma 2.1
> Let $A$ and $B$ be disjoint closed subsets of a normal space $X$, and let
> $$
> \Delta=\{k2^{-n}:n\ge1,\ 0<k<2^n\}
> $$
> be the dyadic rationals in $(0,1)$. There are open sets $\{U_r:r\in\Delta\}$ such that
> $$
> A\subset U_r\subset B^c,
> \qquad
> \overline{U_r}\subset U_s\quad(r<s).
> $$
>
> **Proof sketch.** Normality has the following useful consequence: if $F$ is closed, $G$ is open, and $F\subset G$, then there is an open $U$ such that
> $$
> F\subset U\subset\overline U\subset G.
> $$
> First separate $A$ and $B$ by disjoint open sets $V,W$, and set $U_{1/2}=V$. Then
> $$
> A\subset U_{1/2}\subset\overline{U_{1/2}}\subset W^c\subset B^c.
> $$
> Construct the remaining sets by induction over the dyadic levels. At level $N$, each new number
> $$
> r=(2j+1)2^{-N}
> $$
> lies between two dyadic numbers already treated. Insert $U_r$ between the corresponding closed and open sets so that
> $$
> \overline{U_{j2^{1-N}}}
> \subset U_r
> \subset\overline{U_r}
> \subset U_{(j+1)2^{1-N}}.
> $$
> At the endpoints use $\overline{U_0}=A$ and $U_1^c=B$. Repeating this insertion produces the required nested family.
>
> **Geometric meaning.** The boundaries $\partial U_r$ behave like increasingly ordered contour lines between $A$ and $B$.
> ^lemma-dyadic-open-sets


> [!lemma] Lemma 2.2 (Urysohn)
> If $X$ is normal and $A,B\subseteq X$ are disjoint closed sets, then there exists $f\in C(X,[0,1])$ such that
> $$
> f|_A=0,
> \qquad
> f|_B=1.
> $$
>
> **Proof sketch.** Take the nested open sets from [[#^lemma-dyadic-open-sets]], set $U_1=X$, and define
> $$
> f(x)=\inf\{r:x\in U_r\}.
> $$
> The set in the infimum is nonempty because $U_1=X$. Since $A\subset U_r\subset B^c$ for every $0<r<1$, we obtain $f=0$ on $A$ and $f=1$ on $B$.
>
> To prove continuity, use the nesting property to compute the inverse images of open half-lines:
> $$
> f^{-1}(( -\infty,\alpha))
> =\bigcup_{r<\alpha}U_r,
> $$
> and
> $$
> f^{-1}((\alpha,\infty))
> =\bigcup_{s>\alpha}(\overline{U_s})^c.
> $$
> Both sets are open. Since open half-lines generate the usual topology on $\mathbb R$, continuity follows from [[#^prop-continuity-on-generators]].
>
> **Key point.** The number $f(x)$ records the first contour level $U_r$ that reaches $x$.
> ^lemma-urysohn

> [!theorem] Theorem 2.1 (Tietze)
> Let $X$ be normal, let $A\subseteq X$ be closed, and let $f\in C(A,[a,b])$. Then there exists $F\in C(X,[a,b])$ such that
> $$
> F|_A=f.
> $$
>
> **Proof sketch.** After replacing $f$ by $(f-a)/(b-a)$, it suffices to consider $f:A\to[0,1]$. We construct continuous functions $g_n$ on $X$ satisfying
> $$
> 0\le g_n\le\frac{2^{n-1}}{3^n}
> $$
> on $X$ and
> $$
> 0\le f-\sum_{j=1}^n g_j\le\left(\frac23\right)^n
> $$
> on $A$.
>
> Suppose $g_1,\ldots,g_{n-1}$ have been chosen and put
> $$
> r_{n-1}=f-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}g_j,
> \qquad
> M_n=\left(\frac23\right)^{n-1},
> \qquad
> \delta_n=\frac{M_n}{3}.
> $$
> On $A$, the residual satisfies $0\le r_{n-1}\le M_n$. Its low and high regions
> $$
> B_n=\{r_{n-1}\le\delta_n\},
> \qquad
> C_n=\{r_{n-1}\ge2\delta_n\}
> $$
> are disjoint closed subsets of $X$. By [[#^lemma-urysohn]], choose
> $$
> g_n:X\to[0,\delta_n]
> $$
> with $g_n=0$ on $B_n$ and $g_n=\delta_n$ on $C_n$. This correction never overshoots the residual and reduces its upper bound from $M_n$ to $(2/3)M_n$.
>
> Now define
> $$
> F=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_n.
> $$
> Since
> $$
> \|g_n\|_u\le\frac13\left(\frac23\right)^{n-1},
> $$
> the series converges uniformly. Hence $F$ is continuous. Moreover, $0\le F\le1$ on $X$, and on $A$ the residual tends to zero, so $F|_A=f$. Finally, rescale to $[a,b]$.
>
> **Key point.** Urysohn's lemma supplies continuous corrections on all of $X$, while the geometric error estimate forces their sum to agree exactly with $f$ on $A$.
> ^thm-tietze-extension

> [!corollary] Corollary 2.1
> If $X$ is normal, $A\subseteq X$ is closed, and $f\in C(A)$ is real- or complex-valued, then there exists $F\in C(X)$ such that $F|_A=f$.
>
> **Proof idea.** Treat real and imaginary parts separately. For real-valued $f$, first compress its range by $g=f/(1+|f|)\in C(A,(-1,1))$, extend $g$ using [[#^thm-tietze-extension]], and then use [[#^lemma-urysohn]] to modify the extension so that it avoids the endpoints $\{-1,1\}$. Applying the inverse transform recovers an extension of $f$.
> ^cor-tietze-extension

> [!definition] Definition 2.6
> A topological space $X$ is **completely regular** if it is $T_1$ and, for every closed set $A\subseteq X$ and every $x\notin A$, there exists $f\in C(X,[0,1])$ such that
> $$
> f(x)=1,
> \qquad
> f|_A=0.
> $$
> Completely regular spaces are also called **Tychonoff spaces** or $T_{3\frac12}$ spaces. By [[#^lemma-urysohn]], every normal $T_1$ space is completely regular.
> ^def-completely-regular
